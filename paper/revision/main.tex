\section{Introduction}

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are becoming a practical tool for infrastructure inspection, surveillance, and monitoring, with a growing impact in agriculture, critical infrastructure, and other industrial environments \cite{imran2025environmental,marut2024surveillance}. Power-line inspection constitutes a particularly relevant use case, as it combines long-range navigation, repetitive geometric structures, constrained mission corridors, and the need to react to unforeseen obstacles while maintaining the inspection objectives. The operational experience of companies such as FuVeX has revealed several technological and methodological challenges that remain to be addressed, while also highlighting the importance of simulation-based development.

Testing navigation strategies in representative simulated environments enables systematic assessment under controlled and repeatable conditions, contributes to risk reduction, and facilitates the application of insights gained through simulation to subsequent field operations. Simulation should therefore not be regarded merely as a preliminary verification stage, but rather as an integral component of the design, integration, and maturation of autonomous guidance solutions. This perspective motivates the simulation-oriented methodology adopted in this work.

From a planning point of view, the \deleted{Powerline Inspection}\added{power-line inspection} scenario is \deleted{difficult}\added{challenging} because the vehicle must maintain a global mission structure while responding online to local conflicts. Purely global planners are efficient when the environment is static and fully known, but their performance may degrade under late obstacle appearance or perception uncertainty \cite{debnath2024review,meng2025advances}. On the other hand, fully learned end-to-end policies are harder to audit in safety-critical aeronautical contexts, where traceability and bounded behavior remain important design properties \cite{EASA_AI_Roadmap_2.0}.

The regulatory dimension makes the problem even sharper. Under the UAS regulatory framework of the European Union, the operational core was defined by the Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/947 on the rules and procedures for the operation of unmanned aircraft and the Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/945 on unmanned aircraft systems, while, more recently, EASA AMC/GM material now incorporates the JARUS SORA 2.5 package for the `specific' category \cite{EU2019_947,EU2019_945,JARUS_SORA_2_5_2024,EASA_AMC_GM_SORA25_2025}. Within this framework, the acceptability of a route is not only a matter of geometric collision avoidance but also of limiting exposure of uninvolved people and sensitive ground areas. This means that an autonomous planner should not simply ask whether the UAV \emph{can} fly over a location, but whether it \emph{should} do so in a risk-aware operational sense. The existing literature has already started to encode this concern through urban risk maps, third-party risk models, SORA-aware cost functions, and target-level-of-safety constraints \cite{primatesta2019riskaware,primatesta2020groundrisk,pang2022integrated,tang2023thirdparty,zhang2023groundrisk,heinze2023sora,brassel2025riskaware}. However, most of these works remain strategic route optimizers or city-scale risk planners rather than controller-level local replanners for infrastructure inspection missions already in progress.

The present paper does not claim to encode the full legal tables of open-category separation or the full SORA mitigation calculus within the planner. Instead, it implements the narrower controller-level step that those regulatory mechanisms motivate: live civilian-like detections are converted into temporary protected regions that the UAV should avoid crossing.

In contrast, local path planning approaches generate or adapt trajectories online while the vehicle is moving, allowing immediate reactions to newly perceived environmental elements encountered during flight \cite{debnath2024review,meng2025advances}. More specifically, recent surveys in 2025 and 2026 confirm that the dominant design goals remain collision avoidance, dynamic feasibility, and computational efficiency, while explicit treatment of ground exposure of third-parties is still comparatively rare in runtime planning \cite{merei2025survey,nafees2026review}. The proposed method is explicitly positioned in the online local-replanning class.}

\deleted{A related runtime mechanism is dynamic geofencing, where keep-in and keep-out volumes are inserted into the planner or the traffic-management layer to organize low-altitude operations \cite{stevens2021geofence}. The protected regions studied here differ in that they are generated online from live detections and remain anchored to the active mission waypoint, so the global inspection mission is temporarily inhibited rather than replaced.}

\added{2D formulations represent the operating environment on a plane, commonly through occupancy, grid or cost maps \cite{merei2025survey}. \subsection{Research gap.}
Existing work has addressed both ground-risk considerations and risk-aware path planning for UAV operations. However, these approaches generally optimize geometric avoidance without an explicit uninvolved-people objective, or address ground risk at a strategic route-planning level rather than during the online execution of an inspection mission. Less attention has been given to the local guidance response required when onboard detections indicate that the nominal trajectory may overfly or pass close to an uninvolved person.

The proposed method addresses this operational problem by converting nearby detections into protected local no-fly regions, preserving the active mission waypoint as an input for the avoidance maneuver, and reporting the resulting guidance decisions through the same telemetry.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
At each control iteration, PORCE knows the current vehicle state, the active mission waypoint, and the most recent obstacle list. The method is only concerned with the local subproblem: move from the current position to the current waypoint while avoiding the obstacle subset that lies inside a bounded planning neighborhood.


\added{Planning is formulated in the horizontal plane at the current mission altitude. The protected entities considered in the present implementation are treated as ground-based obstacles, and the avoidance maneuver does not optimize altitude. Consequently, the local PORCE planner preserves the mission altitude and modifies only the horizontal path. This scope choice reduces the online search space and keeps the local planner deterministic and bounded, but it also means that obstacles intersecting the flight level or scenarios requiring vertical avoidance are outside the demonstrated operational envelope.}


Let the vehicle position be \(p_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the current waypoint be \(w_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\), and the perceived obstacles at time \(t\) be \(\mathcal{O}_t = \{o_1,\dots,o_k\}\). Each obstacle is inflated by a hard safety radius \(R_s\), generating a non-flyable region
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{safe}}(o_i) = \{q \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \lVert q - p_{o_i} \rVert_2 \le R_s\}.
\end{equation}

The local free space is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_t = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} \mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{safe}}(o_i).
\end{equation}

In the current implementation, $R_s = 12~m$, which turns safety into a hard geometric constraint instead of a soft penalty. The failsafe proximity check is a different quantity: the 22~m point of no return $d_{\mathrm{PNR}} = R_s + v\tau + v^2/(2a) = 20.5$~m, derived from the same measured terms below; it is not a second exclusion radius. The reaction horizon $D_{\mathrm{react}}=45+2v=61$~m bounds $d_{\mathrm{PNR}}$ with room for replan attempts.

The value is a derived operational setting, not a quantity read from the SORA critical-area model\deleted{: it}\added{. It} is intended to \deleted{absorb the monocular geopositioning error of the perception front-end, the tracking and execution tolerance of the controller, and a regulatory}\added{cover, in a single design} margin, \deleted{all at once.}\added{perception/geopositioning uncertainty and tracking/execution tolerance; these contributions are quantified from the flight logs in this revision: geopositioning error $\varepsilon_{\max}=5.57$~m (p95 3.87~m), end-to-end latency $\tau=0.185$~s, and emergency braking 9.3--10.1~m/s$^2$, all measured from the native DataFlash logs of the three flights V1--V3 (Table~\ref{tab:physical_encounters}). \subsection{Dynamic Triggering Logic}

The method is not permanently active. The controller computes a dynamic reaction horizon
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{react}}(t) =
    \mathrm{clip}\!\left(D_{\mathrm{base}} + k_v \lVert v_t \rVert_2,\,
    D_{\min},\, D_{\max}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert v_t \rVert_2$ is the horizontal ground-speed norm reported by autopilot telemetry, $D_{\mathrm{base}}=45~m$, $k_v=2.0~s$, $D_{\min}=45~m$, and $D_{\max}=80~m$.

The horizon \deleted{can be read}\added{is designed} as a \deleted{stopping-and-reaction}\added{conservative reaction} budget: \deleted{the}\added{a fixed} base distance \deleted{covers the detection-to-decision latency of the 10~Hz control loop plus a conservative braking margin at the 8~m/s cruise speed, and the speed gain enlarges it}\added{is enlarged} linearly with \deleted{the actual}\added{measured} ground speed\deleted{.}\added{ and clipped to the configured operating range. The constants are derived from the measured perception latency and braking dynamics reported above.} The lower clip bound coincides with $D_{\mathrm{base}}$ and \deleted{acts as a guard against negative}\added{enforces a minimum reaction horizon even when the measured ground-speed norm is small} or noisy\deleted{ speed estimates, so the horizon never shrinks below the base distance.}\added{; because $\lVert v_t\rVert_2$ is a norm, it is non-negative by definition.}


If the nearest perceived obstacle satisfies
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{nearest}}(t) \le D_{\mathrm{react}}(t),
\end{equation}
and the minimum replan interval has elapsed, PORCE requests a new local route from the replanning module. Otherwise, the vehicle continues nominal waypoint following.

The trigger is deliberately direction-independent: any obstacle inside the horizon, not only obstacles on the flight path, can request a replan, and the bounded planner itself decides whether a detour is geometrically necessary. Once a valid evasion route exists, the evasion state is latched: it persists until the local route is exhausted, so the avoidance behavior can continue after the obstacle has left the horizon. While the evasion state is active, replanning is allowed at most once per minimum replan interval.

\subsection{Bounded Local A* Planner}

The method uses a local occupancy grid centered on the vehicle. The \deleted{validated }configuration \deleted{uses}\added{used in the reported experiments contains} \(81 \times 81\) cells with $\delta=4~m$ resolution (a 40-cell radius around the vehicle)\deleted{, resulting in a bounded search window that is compatible with online execution inside the control loop.}\added{. This bounds the search space, and is supported by the sensitivity analysis over grid resolution, safety radius and obstacle count (Section~6.2) and by the measured planner runtime (Table~\ref{tab:pilot_montecarlo}).}



\added{Only obstacles within 55~m of the vehicle are handed to the local planner, with the planner input capped at 16 tracks. Two boundary cases are treated explicitly. If the active mission waypoint lies outside the 40-cell local window, the goal is projected to the grid boundary along the waypoint direction. If the projected goal lies inside an inflated obstacle region, the nearest free cell within the configured boundary-search neighborhood is substituted. Conversely, if the start cell is occupied because the vehicle has entered an inflated region before replanning is triggered, the start cell is released to permit an escape route. With $\delta=4~m$, the cell-intersection test can conservatively add up to half a cell diagonal ($\delta\sqrt{2}/2\approx2.8~m$) to the nominal obstacle inflation; therefore the effective discretized exclusion margin may exceed the configured 12~m radius.}
\added{The obstacle that triggers a replan is force-included in the subset even when it lies beyond that gate. The implementation rasterizes a disc mask: a lattice cell is blocked when its square intersects the disc of radius $R_s$ around the obstacle's \emph{estimated continuous position}, so the kept-out set is isotropic and free of the truncation phase hole of a plain $\lceil R_s/\Delta\rceil$ stencil. With $R_s = 12$~m and $\Delta = 4$~m the nominal stencil is $n = \lceil 12/4 \rceil = 3$ cells per direction ($12 = 3\Delta$ exactly).}




Each cell \(c_{ij}\) is labeled as free or occupied according to the inflated obstacle set:
\begin{equation}
    S(c_{ij}) =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if } c_{ij} \cap \left(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \mathcal{F}_t\right) \neq \varnothing,\\
        0, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The local route is computed with A* \cite{hart1968formal} using Euclidean heuristic,
\begin{equation}
    f(n) = g(n) + h(n),
\end{equation}
where \(g(n)\) is the cost of the accumulated path and \(h(n)\) is the Euclidean distance to the local goal. Eight-connectivity is enabled, so the method can combine cardinal (cost $\delta$) and diagonal moves (cost $\delta\sqrt{2}$) while remaining on the grid.

The local goal is the \emph{active mission waypoint}. When that waypoint lies outside the bounded local window it is projected to the grid boundary, and if the projected cell is occupied the nearest free cell is substituted; the local goal can therefore differ from the raw waypoint coordinate while the mission-level target remains unchanged. This detail is important: the local planner is not allowed to redefine the global mission, only to create a temporary bypass that lets the UAV return to the original corridor as soon as the evasion route is exhausted. \added{Using the already active mission waypoint as the local goal also gives PORCE a simple reattachment rule: the local planner does not need to select a new terminal waypoint or solve a second global planning problem after each conflict.}


%\added{The motivation for this strategy is closely related to the power-line inspection use case. The nominal mission trajectory is assumed to be defined in advance according to the inspection requirements and the desired data-acquisition geometry. Therefore, obstacle avoidance should introduce only the deviation needed to resolve a local conflict, while preserving the original trajectory whenever possible. The advantage of the proposed waypoint-based strategy lies not in the use of waypoints itself, which is common in local avoidance methods, but in retaining the active mission waypoint as the recovery objective. This keeps the avoidance maneuver local and allows the UAS to return to the pre-planned trajectory without redefining the mission-level target. In this way, the deviation from the intended data-acquisition path is kept limited to that required to resolve the local conflict.}

\subsection{Failsafe Escalation}

If no valid local route is found, PORCE does not continue nominal flight blindly. The system integration includes an explicit escalation chain with hold, lateral replan, and terminal \texttt{LAND}/\texttt{RTL} actions. This failsafe design is part of the method even when it is not triggered in a particular experiment because it bounds the controller behavior under planner failure. \added{In the reported configuration, a planner failure with the nearest obstacle inside 22~m first commands a 2.5~s hold and retries planning. Repeated failures within a 12~s window escalate the response: the first failures each command a fresh 2.5~s hold at the vehicle's current position, from the third failure the escalation stage is \texttt{HOLD}, at the fifth failure a lateral replan with a 16~m offset is attempted, and at the sixth    failure the terminal action is \texttt{LAND}, with \texttt{RTL} available as a configurable alternative. A 20~s cooldown bounds re-entry into the escalation chain. The counters apply to consecutive planner failures inside the rolling window and are reset by any valid plan; the actions are mutually exclusive and executed in the listed order, and a stale-input skip is handled separately from a planner failure. This chain is specified but was not exercised by induced failures in the reported flights.}

{\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}}
\added{This analysis has no objective of reproducing a specific mission. Its main purpose is to characterize the PORCE behavior when the space difficulty is modified systematically. There are mainly three parameters studied: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Grid cell size:  $c~\in \{4,6,8\}~m$
    \item Safety radius:  $R_s \in \{12,15,18,21\}~m$
    \item Number of obstacles: $N$
\end{itemize}}

\added{A total set of 20 valid computations is made for each combination of the parameters, maintaining the physical environment of $150 \times 150$~m. The nominal target was located on the front-edge of the grid, while its lateral position was varied between trials. Matched relative target positions were preserved across planner configuration to introduce geometric variability without compromising their comparability. This Monte Carlo-type campaign was designed to characterize the sensitivity and operational envelope of the local planner rather than to reproduce a single predefined obstacle configuration.}

\added{The number of obstacles was increased for each ($c,R_s$) pair until the observed Failsafe rate exceeds 20\%. For this representation, the first number of obstacles that exceeds the Failsafe rate is included into the analysis, as it helps to identify the beginning of the degradation region.}

\added{Although obstacle count provides a direct indication of scene density, it does not uniquely represent the geometric difficulty presented to the planner. For a given number of obstacles, the blocked portion of the search space also depends on the adopted safety radius, grid resolution, overlap between inflated obstacle regions, and clipping at the boundaries of the local planning region.}

\added{To provide a common measure of spatial complexity, the effective grid saturation was defined as the percentage of unique grid nodes blocked by the union of the inflated obstacle regions: 
\begin{equation}
    S_{eff}= 100 \frac{N_{blocked}}{N_{grid}}
\end{equation}}

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\captionsetup{justification=centering}
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Main/images/01_effective_grid_saturation_vs_obstacle_number.png}
\caption{Effective grid saturation versus obstacle number}
\label{fig:Seff-nobs}
\end{figure}

\added{Figure \ref{fig:Seff-nobs} shows the evolution of this metric with obstacle count for the evaluated combinations of cell size and safety radius.
This figure illustrates that the same nominal number of obstacles can result in substantially different levels of effective occupancy grid depending on the planner discretization and the adopted safety radius.}

\added{The selected experimental factors explained approximately 92\% of the variability in the effective occupancy of the grid ($R^2=0.916$). This structured relationship confirms that the selected obstacle count, safety radius, grid resolution, and their interactions provide consistent control over the spatial complexity presented to the planner. The subsequent analysis therefore evaluates planner performance as a function of this effective occupancy rather than relying exclusively on the nominal obstacle count.}


\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\captionsetup{justification=centering}
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Main/images/02_failsafe_vs_effective_grid_saturation.png}
\caption{Failsafe rate versus effective grid saturation}
\label{fig:Seef-failsafe}
\end{figure}

\added{An additional effect of grid discretization can be observed for the coarser resolutions. Since obstacle inflation is implemented as $n_s = [R_s/c]$, different nominal safety radii may map to the same obstacle footprint. For $c=8$~m, both $R_s = 12~m$ and $R_s = 15~m$ result in a two-cell inflation, whereas $R_s = 18~m$ and $R_s = 21~m$ result in a three-cell inflation. Consequently, the corresponding effective-occupancy curves overlap. This quantization effect illustrates the loss of spatial discrimination associated with coarse grids and provides an argument to establish a 4~m resolution, for which all evaluated safety radii correspond to distinct discrete inflation levels.}

\added{Once effective grid occupancy had been established as a common descriptor of spatial complexity, planner performance was evaluated directly against this metric. Figure \ref{fig:Seef-failsafe} shows the Failsafe rate as a function of effective saturation of the grid for the evaluated planner configurations. In general, low and moderate occupancy levels are associated with low Failsafe rates. However, the relationship is no one-to-one: configurations with similar global occupancy may exhibit different failure rates depending on how the remaining free space is connected.}

\added{Configurations that share the same discrete inflation level are presented with nearly identical A search spaces, which naturally leads to similar occupancy and planner-outcome statistics. This is why the partial overlap between some curves is not accidental.} 

\added{The effective saturation describes the geometric difficulty, but the number of obstacles remains as useful operational variable. This obstacle count permits interpreting directly how the density level keeps the planner within an acceptable behavior.}

\caption{Failsafe rate versus obstacle number (95\%) Wilson intervals}

\added{Figure \ref{fig:ptueba} presents the observed Failsafe rate as the obstacle count is increased for each evaluated ($c, R_s$) configuration. The horizontal 20 \% threshold is used as an experimental stopping criterion to identify the transition from the predominantly successful operating region towards progressively degraded planner behavior. It should be interpreted only as an experimental criterion for characterizing the operational envelope.}

\added{Because each configuration was evaluated using 20 valid realizations, a single failure corresponds to a 5\% point change in the observed rate. Consequently, small non-monotonic variations between consecutive obstacle counts should be interpreted as Monte Carlo sampling variability rather than as deterministic improvements or degradations.}

\added{These three representations provide complementary views of the same phenomenon: the planner parameters determine how rapidly the free space becomes occupied, the resulting occupancy and other parameters govern the likelihood of Failsafe, and the obstacle-count representation translates this behavior into an intuitive operational envelope.}

\added{The results provide statistical basis for selecting the reference planner configuration used in the subsequent simulation and field validation campaigns. However, this selection is not based only on the performance of this sensitivity analysis, as is explained in section \ref{sec:problem_formulation}.}

\section{\added{Limitations}}

\added{The demonstrated operational envelope must be interpreted narrowly. The physical campaign (three flights, twenty encounters) validates the 12~m exclusion policy operationally: 20/20 encounters kept 14.21--53.04~m ground-truth clearance under the derived configuration ($R_s=12$~m, $\Delta=4$~m, $d_{\mathrm{PNR}}=22$~m). The composed worst-case stopping envelope with guaranteed bank-limit braking (4.57~m/s$^2$) and the measured error tails is 14.9~m; the configured 12~m is covered by the measured emergency braking (9.3--10.1~m/s$^2$) and by the achieved detours, but it is not claimed as a worst-case stopping guarantee and the 2.9~m residual gap is explicit. No failsafe event occurred in any of the three flights (and no manual intervention either), with the escalation chain and the abandonment budgets available throughout.}


\added{The local planner is two-dimensional and preserves the mission altitude; vertical avoidance and 3D obstacles intersecting the flight level are outside the present formulation. The return and landing segment is executed as part of the mission/autopilot logic. The planner-only comparison above uses common kinematic inputs and a common verifier. ESLS, EAOA~\cite{ghaddar2020eaao} and learning-based systems were not run under that protocol. The corresponding reviewer requests are addressed by the sensitivity analysis of the reaction-horizon and obstacle-inflation parameters (Section~6.2), the bias condition of the planner-only comparison (Section~6.3), and the onboard resource log of the physical flights.}
